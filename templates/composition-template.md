## 1. Thesis

One or two sentences. A thesis is a position that a thoughtful reader could disagree with; "Plato thinks the soul is immortal" is not a thesis.

## 2. Why the question matters

One paragraph. Why does it matter, within the TAC curriculum, to settle this question in this way?

## 3. Argument

Three to five clearly labeled sections, each ending with a sentence that names what the section has established.

### 3.1 [Section title — what this section argues]

Topic sentence. Textual evidence with citation. Analysis that tells the reader how the evidence supports the thesis. A concluding sentence naming what has been established.

## 4. Objection

State the strongest objection to your thesis in the form a careful reader would raise it. Use passage evidence that the objector would cite.

## 5. Reply

Answer the objection. A good reply concedes what is genuinely conceded — "the objector is right that X" — and explains why the thesis survives.

## 6. Conclusion

One paragraph. Restate the thesis in light of what the argument and the reply to the objection have established. Do not introduce new evidence here.

## Self-review checklist

Before submitting for grading (self or tutor):

- [ ] The thesis is one or two sentences and is a position a thoughtful reader could deny.
- [ ] Every paraphrase cites a passage.
- [ ] Every quotation is grammatically integrated into the sentence that introduces it.
- [ ] No section ends without a sentence that tells the reader what has been established.
- [ ] The objection is as strong as I can make it, not a strawman.
- [ ] The reply names what the objection gets right before saying why the thesis survives.
- [ ] No new evidence appears in the conclusion.
- [ ] Citations are consistent in style.
