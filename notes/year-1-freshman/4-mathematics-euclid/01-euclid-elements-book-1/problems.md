# Problems — Euclid, *Elements*, Book I

> Target: demonstrate every problem below on paper, with compass and straightedge where a construction is called for, and with a citation to each earlier proposition or postulate used at every step. Solutions in [`solutions.md`](solutions.md). Attempt first, peek second.

## Instructions

- **Constructions** (Euclid's "Problems"): produce the required figure with an explicit sequence of Post. I.1, Post. I.2, Post. I.3 steps. Name every circle, segment, and intersection point.
- **Theorems**: prove the stated claim by a chain of earlier Props., Posts., Defs., or C.N.s, each cited.
- **Check.** Every solution ends with an explicit **"Check."** step: a limiting case, a special case, or a sanity check that the proof really uses what it should.

## Problem set — the foundations

### 1. State and classify

Write out, from memory after the first read:

(a) All 23 definitions of Book I.
(b) All 5 postulates.
(c) All 5 common notions.

Then classify each in a table with columns *Euclid's name*, *"Is it about geometry in particular?"*, *"Is it an enabling-act or a truth-claim?"*

### 2. Postulate 5, in full

Write out Post. I.5 from memory, without paraphrase. Then rewrite it in the contrapositive.

## Problem set — congruence (I.1 – I.26)

### 3. Prop. I.1 — equilateral triangle

On a given finite straight line *AB*, construct an equilateral triangle.

### 4. Prop. I.2 — transporting a length

Place at a given point a straight line equal to a given straight line.

### 5. Prop. I.4 — SAS congruence

If two triangles have two sides equal to two sides and the angle contained by the equal sides equal, then the triangles are equal in all respects.

(Demonstrate by superposition, as Euclid does; in your solution also note what the proof assumes beyond the common notions.)

### 6. Prop. I.5 — *pons asinorum*

In an isosceles triangle, the angles at the base are equal to one another; and if the equal sides are produced, the angles under the base are equal to one another.

### 7. Prop. I.8 — SSS congruence

If two triangles have the three sides equal to the three sides respectively, they have the angles equal which are contained by the equal straight lines.

### 8. Prop. I.16 — exterior angle greater than remote interior

In any triangle, if one side is produced, the exterior angle is greater than either of the interior and opposite angles.

(In your **Check.** step, identify the assumption in the proof that fails on a sphere.)

### 9. Prop. I.20 — triangle inequality

In any triangle, any two sides taken together are greater than the remaining one.

### 10. Prop. I.26 — ASA / AAS congruence

If two triangles have two angles equal to two angles and one side equal to one side, either the side adjoining the equal angles or the side opposite one of the equal angles, then they will also have the remaining sides equal to the remaining sides and the remaining angle equal to the remaining angle.

## Problem set — parallels and angle sum (I.27 – I.32)

### 11. Prop. I.27 — construction of parallels without Post. 5

If a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the alternate angles equal, the two lines are parallel.

### 12. Prop. I.29 — properties of parallels, using Post. 5

If a straight line falls on two parallel straight lines, it makes the alternate angles equal, the exterior angle equal to the interior and opposite angle, and the interior angles on the same side equal to two right angles.

(In your **Check.** step, name exactly where the proof invokes Post. 5.)

### 13. Prop. I.32 — exterior angle and angle sum

In any triangle, the exterior angle is equal to the two interior and opposite angles; and the three interior angles are equal to two right angles.

## Problem set — area and the Pythagorean theorem (I.33 – I.48)

### 14. Prop. I.35 — parallelograms on the same base

Parallelograms on the same base and in the same parallels are equal to one another in area.

### 15. Prop. I.47 — the Pythagorean theorem

In right-angled triangles the square on the side subtending the right angle is equal to the squares on the sides containing the right angle.

Give the construction explicitly; name every earlier proposition used.

### 16. Prop. I.48 — converse of the Pythagorean theorem

If in a triangle the square on one of the sides is equal to the squares on the remaining two sides of the triangle, then the angle contained by the remaining two sides of the triangle is right.

## Optional stretch problems (not in Euclid)

### 17. Playfair's axiom ⇔ Post. I.5

Prove that Post. I.5 is equivalent to Playfair's axiom:

> *Through a point not on a given line, at most one parallel to the given line can be drawn.*

(Requires Post. I.5 in one direction and the neutral props. of Book I in the other.)

### 18. Neutral-geometry boundary

Without appealing to Post. I.5, demonstrate Prop. I.17: the sum of **any two** angles of a triangle is less than two right angles. Explain why this does not contradict I.32, which concerns the sum of **all three** angles. Trace the dependencies of each proof and identify where Post. I.5 enters I.32. Sources: [I.17](https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookI/propI17.html) and [I.32](https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookI/propI32.html).

### 19. Spherical counterexample to I.16

Describe a triangle on the sphere for which the exterior angle is *not* greater than a remote interior angle. What assumption in Euclid's proof of I.16 is violated?
