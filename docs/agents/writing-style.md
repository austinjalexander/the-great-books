# Writing style — compositions and thesis

The TAC syllabus says: "Writing requires both greater completeness and greater precision than does speech." Compositions and the Senior Thesis are where the student learns that precision. This rule sets the standards a tutor should enforce and a writer should self-enforce.

## Structure

Every essay has, in order:

1. **Thesis** — one or two sentences. A position a careful TAC reader could deny.
2. **Why the question matters** — one paragraph, rooted in the curriculum.
3. **Argument** — three to five labeled sections, each ending with a sentence naming what the section has established.
4. **Objection** — the strongest version of the case against, stated with passages the objector would cite.
5. **Reply** — what the objection gets right; why the thesis survives.
6. **Conclusion** — one paragraph. No new evidence.

The Senior Thesis uses the same skeleton, scaled up: sections may be chapters; the objection and reply may be one full chapter each.

## Paragraph discipline

- **One topic sentence per paragraph.** If you cannot underline it, the paragraph is not doing its job.
- **Evidence-then-analysis.** After a quotation or a paraphrase, at least one full sentence tells the reader *how* the passage supports the thesis. Never let a citation stand alone.
- **Grammatically integrated quotation.** "Plato says 'knowledge is recollection' at *Meno* 81d" is not integrated. Rewrite so the quoted phrase fits the syntax of your sentence.
- **No floating pronouns across paragraphs.** If the referent of "he" or "it" is not in the same paragraph, name it.

## Citation

- **Primary texts.** Use the work's own reference system first (Bekker, Stephanus, book-chapter-verse, part-question-article, book-proposition), then give the edition in the bibliography.
- **Style.** Chicago notes-and-bibliography or MLA. Pick one per essay and stay consistent. The thesis stays in one system across all drafts.
- **First citation, full form.** Author, *Title*, trans. [name] (Place: Publisher, Year), [citation]. Subsequent citations: short form.
- **Scripture.** Book chapter:verse, translation name parenthetical on first citation.
- **No ibid. without verification.** Every ibid. must refer to the immediately preceding citation of the same work.

## Voice

- Plain. Not simple, not ornamented. The model is the prose of Aquinas in English translation: patient, unhurried, unafraid of "therefore."
- First person is allowed but rare. "I argue that" is fine; "I think" is weaker than the claim it introduces.
- Avoid hedging padding: "it seems that," "arguably," "perhaps it could be said that." Commit.
- Avoid rhetorical questions. If it is a question, ask it in the thesis; if it is a claim, state it.

## What a tutor grades

Use the `grade-composition` skill. The rubric has five dimensions:

1. **Thesis** — deniable, specific, sustained throughout.
2. **Evidence** — every claim about a text has a citation; passages are chosen well.
3. **Structure** — sections labeled; each ends with an earned conclusion; objection and reply are genuine.
4. **Prose** — topic sentences, integrated quotations, plain voice, consistent citation.
5. **Originality of question** — especially in the Senior Thesis: is the question one the authors themselves frame?

## Anti-patterns

- **Strawman objection.** If the objection is one a careful reader would not raise, strengthen it until it is one, or pick a different objection.
- **Concession that kills the thesis.** A reply that gives up what the thesis claims, then keeps claiming the thesis, is an unrecognized retreat. If the objection wins, revise the thesis.
- **Evidence-dumping.** Long quotations without analysis are placeholders, not arguments.
- **Commentary-first.** Citing Gilson or Pieper to establish a claim *about Aquinas* before citing Aquinas is backward. Primary text first, secondary to clarify.
- **Over-scoping.** An essay that tries to answer "what is justice?" fails. An essay that answers "does Plato's *Republic* V argument for equal education of men and women presuppose the tripartite soul of Book IV?" can succeed.

## Self-review checklist

Copy into every draft until it is second nature:

- [ ] The thesis is one or two sentences and could be denied by a careful reader.
- [ ] Every paraphrase cites a passage.
- [ ] Every quotation is grammatically integrated.
- [ ] Every section ends with a sentence naming what it has established.
- [ ] The objection is the strongest I can construct.
- [ ] The reply names what the objection gets right before answering.
- [ ] No new evidence appears in the conclusion.
- [ ] Citations are consistent in style throughout.
- [ ] Primary text precedes secondary literature on every claim.
